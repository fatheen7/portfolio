Google MapsThe last 100 metres

Maps gets the rider to the building. The handover still starts with a phone call.

Independent case study

Target user
Delivery riders arriving at gated complexes and buildings with several entrances, and the people receiving the order.
Pain point
Maps ends the trip at the building, not at the right gate, so the handover starts with a phone call and a walk around the complex.
Gap
Maps can show where entrances are, and Address Descriptors describe the area. Neither says which entrance to use for this delivery, and delivery apps only receive a point on the map.
Solution
The receiver confirms the delivery entrance when they share a location. The rider is guided to that gate, and delivery apps receive it through the Maps Platform.
North Star
Time from arrival to handover, for the slowest 10% of deliveries
Guardrails
Time to share a location, wrong or unsafe entrances, and privacy reports

Chennai MetroThe ride home after a match

Paying for the metro is solved for regulars. It breaks at the busiest moment: everyone leaving a match at once.

Independent case study · Field teardown

Target user
Fans leaving a big match at Chennai Central, especially first-time riders without a ticket on their phone.
Pain point
At the busiest moment of the night they queue twice: at the ticket counter, then again at the gates.
Gap
Paying is solved for regular riders through WhatsApp and app QR tickets. Event nights are not, except for sponsored IPL rides.
Solution
Make the match ticket the metro ticket wherever a sponsor pays; otherwise send a "book your ride home" link with every big-event ticket.
North Star
Share of post-match riders who enter without visiting a counter
Guardrails
Failed QR scans at the gates and cost per event

Inside the case

  • Queue model
  • Benchmarking
  • RICE
  • Metrics

WrinklIroning, ordered on WhatsApp

Ironing is a small chore with a large coordination cost. Could it be as easy to order as anything else?

Early-stage venture

Target user
Households who iron every week or need a batch at short notice, and the neighbourhood dhobi shops that would do the pressing.
Pain point
Ironing is a weekly chore with a coordination cost: finding a shop, arranging pickup and return, and guessing the price.
Gap
Quick commerce reached food and groceries, not ironing. Local shops can press fast, but none of them collect and deliver.
Solution
Ironing only, ordered on a WhatsApp bot, with Wrinkl handling pickup and return in booked slots and the price shown before you confirm.
North Star
Orders returned pressed, on time, each week
Guardrails
Re-press requests and delivery cost per order

Entrepreneurship · Field note

I expected a no from the dhobi shop

Wrinkl never reached a trial. The conversation that came closest taught me the most.

Wrinkl ran from January to October 2025. I owned the research, the pricing, the ordering prototype and a WhatsApp bot.

When I went to dhobi shops to ask about a trial, I expected a no. I was a stranger asking a working shop to change how it took orders. One owner said yes. She was glad to help someone starting out in her line of work, and far more open than I had prepared for.

I had prepared for objections, not kindness. It changed how I see the supply side. The people who run these shops are partners, not obstacles to route around, and a plain, honest ask went further than any pitch.

The trial never started. When the team's priorities shifted, I set Wrinkl aside before it could run. That is why this site makes no claim about orders, customers or revenue.

What I keep is the order of work. I built the ordering prototype before my first real conversation with a shop. Next time, the conversation comes first.

Product Thinking · Essay

A good idea is not a plan

The mistake I made at the start of Wrinkl, and the one most first-time founders make.

When I started Wrinkl, I made the mistake most first-time founders make. I thought it would be easy because the idea was good. Nobody offered the service, and nobody seemed close to building it.

I read the empty market as proof the idea was strong. It only told me nobody had done it yet. It did not tell me why, or whether people wanted it.

Now I ask what makes an idea hard before I ask what makes it good, and I look for the cheapest way to test the hard part first.

Product Management · Field note

One finding, three readers

How I write the same research for a PM, an engineer and a ticket.

I write the same finding three ways. For my PM: short, recommendation first. For an engineer: exact about how things work. In a ticket: no hedging, so nobody has to guess what was decided.

I also keep solutioning out of reference documents and tickets. A reference document says what is true. A recommendation belongs in the message to stakeholders, where it can be argued with.

It took me a while to see these are not three styles. They are one habit: ask what the reader has to do next, and write for that.
